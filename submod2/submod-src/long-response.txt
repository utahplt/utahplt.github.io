Thank you, reviewers, for your feedback! I quote below parts of your
reviews to some specific questions and comments, limited by space.

> ----------------------- REVIEW 1 ---------------------
> The semantics is given algorithmically [...]

I think the example of using set membership instead of `resolve' (see
below) may be a good example of how the model can be made more
declarative. Do you have other examples to suggest?

There is a bit of concrete syntax in the model, but that's because
it's meant to explain macro expansion (i.e., parsing), which in turn
enables other kinds of phases (like documentation time). I think the
concrete syntax is otherwise minimal. Again, though, suggestions may
help me see what you mean.

> You say both "loading a module" and "running a module". [...]

I have used the two terms in imprecise and almost interchangable ways
in the less formal parts of the paper, and the model covers only the
running part (because finding declarations on demand, which is the
essence of loading, seems relatively straightforward). The
presentation should be improved.

> In page 2, it is written "The meaning of [...]" Do you
> simply mean [...]

Yes, and this is a good example of where the informal part could be
clearer. The model covers this in the `run' metafunction and the
`visit' metafunction (the latter shown in the full model and described
in the paper as being like `run').

> In page 3, it is written "When a module is used in multiple phases, 
> [...] I couldn't figure out
> where this is implemented in the formalization in section 5.

In Figure 4, the `run' metafunction checks for an existing instance of
a module at a given phase, and it attaches a phase to every
instantiation of a definition within a module.

> page 7, left, para 1. What is a definition's binding phase?

The intent is to refer to the phase where a `define-syntax' form
binds, as opposed to the phase (one higher) where its right-hand side
resides.

> In figure 4, I think you can omit the shift metafunction, [...]

The `eval' function is fairly standard, while handling phase shifts is
not. In particular, it would be easy to get phases wrong by threading
a phase through `eval' instead of applying a shift at the right place
--- akin to accidentally implementing dynamic scope by threading an
environment through an interpreter instead of forming closures.

> I think the detailed handling of syntax objects is irrelevant, [...]

I have left out most macro machinery, but I don't know how to provide
a coherent account of macro expansion without using syntax objects to
the minimal degree they are used in this paper. In particular, there's
no way to talk about bindings for different phases without something
like syntax objects.

> In the definition of the resolve metafunction [...]

The phase is always the same. I see why the notation of figure 7 is
confusing. The `ph' is bound at the first use on the left-hand side of
the rule, and other uses (whether on the left or right of a side
condition) imply matching. This is normal Redex notation, but it is
not standard. At the same time, the current use of `resolve' may be a
bad compromise between using set membership directly and leaving
`resolve' as a hook for a model that has more complete macro
machinery.

> page 9, right: "the bindings inherited by the submodule
> [...]". Please elaborate.

The "submodule's phase nesting" is intended to refer back to the
next-to-last paragraph of section 4, which explains how a submodule
can be nested under `begin-for-syntax'. That connection should be made
clearer.

> ----------------------- REVIEW 2 ---------------------
> Perhaps the reader's
> understanding would by help by also providing a comparison between,
> say, the ML module system (i.e., a language *without* macros but
> *with* modules)

Racket's model system without macros is indeed little different from
ML structures. As the related-work section notes, however, nested ML
structures run whenever the enclosing structure runs, while submodules
are not so constrained. Inner classes in Java are even closer, as far
as non-extensible languages go.

> Section 2 begins to be difficult to follow when it introduces
> `for-template`.  I understand `for-syntax` [...]

Your analogy sounds reasonable.

> I remain confused about the relationship between submodules and
> phases after reading this in section 3:

I think this is a bad transition in the exposition.  "This treatment"
is meant to refer to the specific treatment of names like `main' and
`test'; that protocol could be useful without phases, such as in a
variant of ML modules where nested structures can be independent of
enclosing structures. The rest of the paragraph is intended to bridge
to the rest of the section, where macros and phases come into play to
leverage of submodules in a greater way.

> Is it really possible for Racket programmers to define their own
> phases as the paper advertises, [...] baked-in language forms like
> `for-doc` that section 3 describes?

The `for-doc' form is implemented in `scribble/srcdoc', not baked-in.

> - In section 2, why switch from .scm to .lsp [...]

Yes: The ".lsp" examples sketch the way Common Lisp works (but not
exactly syntactically right), while ".scm" sketches the way old Scheme
implementations worked (again, not exactly syntactically right for any
Scheme implementation).

> - In section 4, it looks as though in the Redex model one has to
>   manually annotate imports and exports with the phase numbers [...]

I think of the annotations are more like types on module
boundaries. They annotations can be inferred, but it changes the
capabilities of the language in subtles ways. See the related-work
section's discussion of "implicit phasing" (Ghuloum and Dybvig) for
more on why we stick with explicit phasing.

> - Is the Redex model capable of modeling side-effecting module bodies

The current model does not include side-affecting operations, but they
are easily accomodated in the usual way by threading the store through
the `eval' metafunction.

>  [...] [Flatt 2002] [...] the
>   contribution of this paper is beyond the previous one.

The contribution is the design and implementation of submodules. The
original design and paper did not include submodules.


> ----------------------- REVIEW 3 ---------------------
> specific to Racket and quite unclear to me if
> these ideas can be made useful outside this context. 

Racket is on the cutting edge of one form of language extensibility,
so the work that we do may not immediately apply to other languages.
Some of our recent work also shows how Scheme techniques can apply to
a language with infix syntax (GPCE'12). Furthermore, Scheme-style
macros are starting find their way into Scala and JavaScript (see
"sugar.js" or Eich's recorded keynote at StrangeLoop'12).

> I worry that this is essentially creating a
> configuration system that's no easier to understand than a complex
> nest of #ifdef's in a language with a C-like pre-processor.

The module and macro system described in the paper is the foundation
of Racket's ability to express sophisticated language constructs, such
as contracts, Scribble, and Typed Racket. No one is building anything
like contracts, Scribble, or Typed Racket with the C pre-processor.
